# Buy arcade guts, keep your own glow

**Yes for the mechanism, no for the whole button.** Garett can buy a press mechanism that feels like an arcade cabinet and is rated for tens of millions of presses, but no large button on sale ships with addressable light. Every good option therefore keeps Pax Buttonica's own WS2812s and the engine's effects. The best-supported pick is a **100 mm pop'n-music-style arcade dome fitted with an Omron V-10-1A4 microswitch** over a steel return spring of 100–200 g. The V-10-1A4 is the 100 gf snap switch that Sanwa's pop'n button sets ship with, and Omron's datasheet rates it at **50 million** mechanical operations. The dome can be the genuine Sanwa OBSA-100UMQ if six can be found (about USD 30–51 each, sold out at the US shops checked) or a roughly CAD 15 generic dome with the same switch fitted. Close behind come two others. A **60 mm dome** fits today's 72 × 80 mm footprint (the box gets taller) and is the only big button with a published WS2812 retrofit. The **Kailh "Big Switch"** (55.8 mm body, 14 mm travel) is rated down to 10 µA, so it works on the ESP32's internal pull-up, but Canada has none in stock until at least late October. Industrial RGB pushbuttons are out: the biggest RGB face found is 30 mm, and they show one colour at a time. Wiring any of these takes three wires and one resistor. The switch's COM terminal goes to GND and its NO terminal to G1, which the button firmware already reads as the big button. A 1 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V completes it, because arcade microswitches have silver contacts that are specified for more current than the internal pull-up passes. Nobody has published an edge-slap test of any big dome. So the first purchase should be a small test order, about two domes of each size, a handful of Omron switches and one Big Switch if stock arrives, and it should be slapped, held and lit before anything is bought six times.

## Every big button on sale glows one colour at a time

The four research tracks (arcade domes, arcade-button switches, industrial pushbuttons and keyboard-style switches) agree on one point: **above 30 mm, light and mechanism are sold separately.** Big domes ship with a single lamp. Adafruit's 100 mm dome has a 12 V LED and warns that "given the massive size and shallow depth of the button, it doesn't illuminate the entire button face evenly" ([Adafruit 1185 PDF](https://mm.digikey.com/Volume0/opasdata/d220001/medias/docus/2550/1185_Web.pdf)). Sanwa's OBSA-100UMQ takes a #194 wedge bulb or comes with no lamp at all ([GameRepair.info](https://gamerepair.info/parts/95_sanwa_illumintated_dome_push_button)). Industrial makers do sell RGB, but the largest RGB face found is **30 mm** (APEM AV 30, Onpow GQ30, Schurter MCS 30). Each is one three-colour LED behind a thin ring, driven by three on/off or dimming lines ([Onpow catalogue](https://onpowbutton.com/uploads/cbf00c4e889cf3e37bc6068b6ddaa3c33.pdf); [Schurter MCS 30](https://www.schurter.com/en/datasheet/MCS_30)). Such a button can show any player colour and whole-ring `pulse`, `flash` and a time-cycled `rainbow`, but nothing that moves. The hex firmware's `ripple`, `spin` and `rings` would all collapse to a fade. The industrial heads big enough to slap, EAO's Series 45 mushrooms at 40 and 60 mm (up to 10 million cycles, IP69K), light in one fixed colour ([EAO Series 45 brochure](https://media.graphassets.com/ynqgxnNST6lA2dsstsbX)). The only bought pushbutton with a WS2812 inside is the hobby-grade **ChromaTek**, a 19 mm steel button whose specs are all seller text ([Amazon.ca ChromaTek](https://www.amazon.ca/ChromaTek-Full-Color-MomentaryPush-Arduino-Raspberry/dp/B0988V21SM)). Hall-effect "magnetic" buttons have no contacts to wear, but they exist only inside complete controllers with their own USB boards. One example is Zenaim's kit: "exceeding 100 million" presses in testing, ¥29,700, Japan-only so far ([Zenaim](https://zenaim.com/en/blogs/news/250915)).

So the answer is to **buy the mechanism and keep the WS2812 light.** That also suits the project's ground rules: the engine, `deriveOutputs` and the wire protocol stay untouched, and only the button's physical stack changes.

The mechanism is where today's button is weak. The cap presses the AtomS3 Lite's own small face switch through printed flexures. Two of three flexures snapped on 2026-09-28 and the bars cracked again on 2026-09-30 (`hardware/shell/README.md`, `hardware/HANDOFF.md`). Commercial big buttons split that one job three ways. In Sanwa's pop'n buttons and the DJ-DAO POPOLLER controller, a full-size **Omron V-10-1A4 snap microswitch** sits under the dome and a separate coil spring of 100–400 g lifts and centres it ([rhythm-cons wiki](https://rhythm-cons.wiki/parts/buttons/100-circular-dome-buttons/); [Tech-Critter POPOLLER review](https://www.tech-critter.com/gamo2-popoller-popn-music-controller-review/)). The microswitch's internal snap spring makes the click, the coil spring keeps the dome level, and the plunger tube keeps it straight. The POPOLLER review describes what happens when the balance is off: with too light a spring "the button can wobble as it is being pressed... can actually catch against the housing and get stuck," so the reviewer advises against going below about 100 g ([Tech-Critter](https://www.tech-critter.com/gamo2-popoller-popn-music-controller-review/)). Rated life is not the limit. Even a 1-million-press switch outlasts a thousand games at a few hundred presses each. For a table button that is slapped and held, the failures that matter are **wobble, sticking and tarnish on the contacts**. The ranking below is therefore mostly about the plunger guide and the contact metal.

### 1. A 100 mm pop'n dome with an Omron V-10-1A4

The 100 mm dome is the arcade industry's own button for being hit all day. It is the button in Konami's pop'n music cabinets ([Coin-Op Express](https://www.coinopexpress.com/products/parts/push_button/Sanwa-Button-OBSA-100UMQ-8212.html)), and rhythm-game builders sort it into three grades ([rhythm-cons wiki](https://rhythm-cons.wiki/parts/buttons/100-circular-dome-buttons/)). The **Sanwa OBSA-100UMQ** is the original: Bluespring's pop'n set ships it with Omron V-10-1A4 switches for home controllers and with Sanwa springs at 100 and 400 g, and the seller says it gives "a crisp sound when pressed" **[seller]** ([Bluespring Express](https://bluespringexpress.net/en-us/products/popn-music-sanwa-button-set-obsa-100umq)). The Korean **Samducksa CWB 401**, about USD 9, pairs a 65 g switch with a 200 g spring and is "notably lighter" **[community]** ([rhythm-cons wiki](https://rhythm-cons.wiki/parts/buttons/100-circular-dome-buttons/)); Betson stocks it in the US ([Betson](https://www.betsonparts.com/amusement-redemption/button-switch-cwb-401-white.html)). The **generic Chinese dome**, sold as Adafruit, SparkFun, Abra, EG Starts and Amazon's "massive arcade button", shares one set of specs across those listings and almost certainly comes from one factory. Builders call its springs and switches "extremely heavy... upwards of 500-600g total" **[community]** ([rhythm-cons wiki](https://rhythm-cons.wiki/parts/buttons/100-circular-dome-buttons/)). One builder measured about 130 g on the switch alone, took the spring out, let the switch return the cap, and found "the buttons feel quite good" **[community]** ([macoy.me](https://macoy.me/blog/diy/PopnCon)).

**Why it survives.** The switch carries the life rating. Omron's datasheet gives **50,000,000 mechanical operations minimum**, a 0.98 N (100 gf) maximum operating force on the "4" grade, and contact bounce under 1 ms **[datasheet]** ([Omron V datasheet](https://omronfs.omron.com/en_US/ecb/products/pdf/en-v.pdf)). The "10,000,000 cycles" line on Sanwa and generic listings is seller copy that names no switch maker **[seller]** ([SparkFun](https://www.sparkfun.com/big-dome-pushbutton-red.html); [Abra](https://abra-electronics.com/electromechanical/switches/arcade-game-switches/pbs-100mm-gn.html)). The dome itself has no life or impact rating anywhere, and Bluespring warns that new Sanwa domes "can suffer from sticking when they are new from the factory." Its advice is to start on 400 g springs and drop to 100 g after wear-in **[seller]** ([Bluespring](https://bluespringexpress.net/en-us/products/popn-music-sanwa-button-set-obsa-100umq)).

The safe starting recipe is the arcade pop'n standard of a **100 g switch on a 200 g spring** ([rhythm-cons pop'n page](https://rhythm-cons.wiki/controllers/pop-n-music/pop-n-music/)). Don't go below 100 g, and don't overtighten the panel nut, which pinches the plunger and is a known cause of sticking ([RetroOnly](https://retroonly.com/how-to-fix-sticky-arcade-buttons/)).

**Fit.** Every 100 mm dome mounts in a **24 mm hole** on a threaded body. Listings give about **70–72 mm** of height without saying whether the dome is included. Switch tabs are 4.8 mm and lamp tabs 6.3 mm ([SparkFun](https://www.sparkfun.com/big-dome-pushbutton-red.html); [Abra](https://abra-electronics.com/electromechanical/switches/arcade-game-switches/pbs-100mm-gn.html)). It does not fit the 72 × 80 box. The button becomes the dome on a printed base a little wider than 100 mm, with the Atom, Rider and cell standing beside the 24 mm stem instead of under a cap. Buy **white** domes: a red or blue dome would filter the other players' colours. That follows from basic optics; no source tested it.

**Price and where.** Sanwa sells at USD 29.99–37.99 at US retailers but was sold out at Video Games New York ([VGNY](https://videogamesnewyork.com/sanwa-obsa-100umq-button-1x/)). Bluespring's nine-button set (USD 367.59) was also sold out. The wiki lists ¥5,038–7,535 each, so Japanese exporters are the likely source. Six would come to about USD 180–300 before shipping and duty, roughly CAD 250–420 at an assumed 1.37 CAD per USD.

The generic dome is easy to get in Canada. Amazon.ca lists one at **CAD 15.39**, a price seen only in a search snippet ([Amazon.ca B0C9LJND6T](https://www.amazon.ca/Massive-Replacement-Convexity-Illuminated-Operated/dp/B0C9LJND6T)). Abra Electronics, which displays CAD, lists USD 10.95 ([Abra](https://abra-electronics.com/electromechanical/switches/arcade-game-switches/pbs-100mm-gn.html)). Adafruit's white 1187 is USD 9.95 ([Adafruit 1187](https://www.adafruit.com/product/1187)), and DigiKey carries Adafruit's 100 mm domes ([DigiKey 1185](https://www.digikey.com/en/products/detail/adafruit-industries-llc/1185/6817190)). Elmwood, the Canadian Adafruit reseller, is sold out and now orders through PiShop.ca ([Elmwood](https://elmwoodelectronics.ca/products/massive-arcade-button-with-led-100mm-white)).

### 2. A 60 mm dome that keeps today's footprint

The 60 mm dome is only 4 mm wider than the current 56 mm cap and its body drops through a 28–30 mm hole, so it can crown the existing 72 × 80 box with the electronics around the stem. The box gets taller, though: the stem hangs well below the 50 mm v2 box. Adafruit's 60 mm cap goes from 15.2 mm tall to 12 mm when pressed, about 3 mm of travel, and has a **52.5 mm stem including the switch** **[seller]** ([Adafruit 1190](https://www.adafruit.com/product/1190)). Arcade Express lists its convex 60 mm at 67 mm tall overall ([Arcade Express 60 mm](https://www.arcadexpress.com/en/arcade-pushbuttons/276-550-rounded-convex-led-illuminated-arcade-button-60mm.html)).

It is the **only big button with a published WS2812 retrofit**. scruss hot-glued an 8-LED ring into the underside cavity of a "tall" 60 mm white cap. The cavity is "about 45 mm in diameter and only a few millimetres deep." The wires went through the gaps where the lamp clips had been, and the LEDs were "quite happy with 3.3 V logic" **[community]** ([scruss](https://scruss.com/blog/2022/03/13/adding-rgb-leds-to-an-illuminated-arcade-button/); mirrored by [Elmwood](https://elmwoodelectronics.ca/blogs/news/adding-rgb-leds-to-an-illuminated-arcade-button)). The NeoHEX, 31 mm across flats and about 36 mm with ears, fits that cavity by width; its thickness with the connector needs measuring.

Its weaknesses are the generic ones. The switch is an unnamed "arcade microswitch," so plan to fit an Omron V in its place. The body cracks when drilled ("the plastic of the button cracks easily" **[community]**, [hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/174102/log/181863-hacking-an-arcade-button-to-insert-a-neopixel-ring)), so wires go through existing gaps. Adafruit's flat-top 60 mm is not the domed "tall" one scruss used, and nothing at this size carries a cycle rating. Adafruit's white 1192 is **USD 5.95** and in stock ([Adafruit 1192](https://www.adafruit.com/product/1192)). Arcade Express is in Spain and out of stock, and no Canadian stock was confirmed.

### 3. The Kailh Big Switch, if stock returns

The Big Switch is a 4×-scale Cherry MX keyboard switch. Kailh's datasheet gives a **55.8 mm square body, 61 mm tall**, **14 ± 4 mm of total travel** with 4.8 mm before it triggers, a **280 ± 60 gf** operating force, a 56 × 56.2 mm mounting cutout, **5,000,000 mechanical cycles**, and a 3 kgf static load held for 60 s without damage **[datasheet]** ([Kailh datasheet](https://cdn-shop.adafruit.com/product-files/5306/5306_CPG151101D228_Burnt_Orange_stem.pdf)). Electrically it is the best fit of anything here. It is rated from "10μA DC min" and "2V DC min," so the ESP32's internal pull-up alone is within spec and **no external resistor is needed**. Bounce reaches 10 ms by end of life, so the firmware's debounce should stay at 10 ms or more.

It comes as a Dark Yellow linear, a Burnt Orange tactile (290 g peak) and a Pale Blue clicky **[seller]** ([NovelKeys](https://novelkeys.com/products/the-big-switch-series)). One reviewer found it smooth, with a clear polycarbonate top housing that has "a rather narrow but seemingly functional LED slot." He also found it loud ("incredible chaos") and noted hard housing collisions at top and bottom **[review]** ([ThereminGoat](https://www.theremingoat.com/blog/novelkeys-x-kailh-big-dark-yellow-switch-review)). Keybumps' "50 Million" lifespan contradicts Kailh's own 5 million and appears copied from normal-size switches ([Keybumps](https://keybumps.com/switches/nk-big-switch-burnt-orange.html)). There is no impact rating. A hard palm slap can briefly exceed the 3 kgf static figure, so slap resistance is unproven.

**Fit and light.** The 56 mm cutout fits the 72 × 80 footprint, but the switch stands about 41 mm above the mounting plate before its keycap, so the box gets taller. A ø15.4 mm post and the two pins sit at the centre, exactly where the NeoHEX's middle LEDs are. Light has to come from a ring of LEDs around the post. Keebio's BAMFK-1 board does this with 8 upward and 8 underglow RGB LEDs, though that detail comes from a search snippet ([Keebio](https://keeb.io/products/big-switch-pcb)). No source says whether the bundled keycap passes light. Garett's printed caps would carry over: Kailh itself promoted a 3D-printed lit keycap for the switch ([Kailh news](https://www.kailhswitch.com/news/kailh-big-switch-god-of-keycappie-19304444.html)).

**Availability is the problem.** DigiKey.ca lists the tactile at **CA$38.96, 0 in stock, one unit expected 29 Oct 2026** ([DigiKey.ca](https://www.digikey.ca/en/products/detail/adafruit-industries-llc/5306/15794639)). NovelKeys is sold out until "Q1 2027", a date that has already slipped once ([NovelKeys](https://novelkeys.com/products/the-big-switch-series)). Adafruit no longer stocks the linear ([Adafruit 5305](https://www.adafruit.com/product/5305)). Six from DigiKey.ca would be CA$234 once stock exists.

### 4. Keep the printed body, buy only the switch and spring

This is the smallest change. It keeps the 72 × 80 box, the NeoHEX centred under the 56 mm cap, and the printable caps from `make_caps.py`. The Atom's face switch and the printed flexures are replaced by an Omron V microswitch and a steel coil spring, and the printed parts are left only to guide the cap, carrying no bending load.

To keep the hex in the middle, put the switch off to one side. Omron's hinge-lever versions trigger with less force at the lever tip: 0.59 N for the V-102-1A4, and 0.34 N for the long-lever V-103-1A4 with up to 9 mm of travel before it triggers **[datasheet]** ([Omron V datasheet](https://omronfs.omron.com/en_US/ecb/products/pdf/en-v.pdf)). One DIY quiz-buzzer builder found that a single switch missed edge presses and moved to three switches in a triangle **[snippet]** ([Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Game-Show-Buzzer-System/)). Two or three V switches wired side by side to one pin is a cheap hedge against that.

A gold-contact alternative in the same full-size footprint is Cherry/ZF's D41 "Crosspoint" grade. It needs 45 cN in its light-force version and is rated at 10 million operations with a POM actuator, but only 1 million with a PET one **[datasheet]** ([Cherry D4 datasheet](https://docs.rs-online.com/6cb9/0900766b809e0c33.pdf)). Its current availability was not checked.

The risk is that the guide is still printed PLA, the part most likely to rock or wedge. A bought dome's plunger tube is exactly what the other options pay for.

### 5. A keyboard switch under a printed cap

Kailh Box switches are rated "up to 80M cycles", sealed to IP56, with gold contacts, 45–60 gf and 3.6 mm of travel **[maker]** ([Kailh](https://www.kailh.net/products/kailh-box-switch-set)). Cherry's MX2A Red is rated at more than 100 million keystrokes, 45 cN and 4.0 mm, with gold "Crosspoint" contacts **[maker]** ([Cherry MX2A Red](https://www.cherry.de/en-us/product/mx2a-red)). Both work on the internal pull-up, and both are sold in Canada ([Keychron.ca](https://keychron.ca/products/kailh-box-switch-set); [Shockport](https://shockport.ca/)).

They rank last because they give the most life and the least slap. Four millimetres of light, linear travel under a 56 mm cap is a short clack, not an arcade plunge. One switch also rocks under edge slaps unless keyboard-style wire stabilizers or a guide hold the cap level ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2020/08/20/print-your-way-to-keyboard-stability/)). The 30 mm arcade buttons built on such switches, like the Qanba Gravity (seller-rated at 70 million, CA$5.99 at [Canadian Joysticks](https://www.canadianjoysticks.com/qanba-gravity-solid-colour-30mm-mechanical-pushbutton-black/)), are too small to slap on their own.

## Three wires and one resistor connect any of them

**The switch.** A microswitch has three tabs: **COM** (common), **NO** ("normally open", joined to COM only while the button is pressed) and **NC** (the opposite, left unconnected). Wire COM to GND and NO to **G1**, the white wire of the AtomS3 Lite's Grove socket. The button firmware already treats G1 as the big button, with the internal pull-up switched on (`BIG_BUTTON_PINS = {1, 8, 41}` in `firmware/button/config.h`), so no code change is needed. The NeoHEX uses that socket's yellow wire (G2) for data, so the switch needs M5's Unit Hub splitter, which shell v0.6 already used, or a spliced lead. G1 has a bonus: today's face button (G41) cannot wake the S3 from sleep, but GPIO 0–21 can, so a bought switch on G1 makes deep sleep possible again (`config.h`).

**The pull-up, in plain terms.** The ESP32 holds G1 at 3.3 V through a weak internal resistor of tens of kilohms (check Espressif's datasheet for the exact figure). Pressing the switch joins the pin to ground, and the chip sees the voltage drop. Through that weak resistor only a trickle of current crosses the contacts, and silver contacts are specified for more: Omron's V series gives a reference minimum load of "DC5V 160mA" **[datasheet]** ([Omron V](https://omronfs.omron.com/en_US/ecb/products/pdf/en-v.pdf)), Omron's smaller D2FC gives 5 V/1 mA **[datasheet]** ([Omron D2FC](https://mm.digikey.com/Volume0/opasdata/d220001/medias/docus/5389/D2FC_Datasheet_EN.pdf)), and Sanwa rates its own button switch at 5 V/10 mA **[seller]** ([Paradise SW-68](https://paradisearcadeshop.com/products/sanwa-sw-68)). Silver grows a thin tarnish film, and a trickle may not break through it, which shows up years later as presses that go missing. The fix is one **1 kΩ resistor from G1 to the Atom's 3.3 V pin**. Never use the 5 V rail: that would put 5 V on a pin built for 3.3 V. About 3.3 mA then flows only while the button is held, comfortably above the D2FC's 1 mA floor. Next to the **46–120 mA** the button already draws at 5 V for its hex (measured, `hardware/HANDOFF.md`), that is nothing on the battery. Gold-contact parts (the Big Switch, the Cherry D41, MX and Box switches) need no resistor.

**The light.** The light keeps today's three wires: 5 V and GND from the Lipo Rider, and data on G2. The real choice is where the LEDs sit, and each place has a cost. **In the moving cap**, as scruss did, the light is brightest and closest to the dome, but the wires flex on every slap: use fine silicone wire (he used 28 AWG), leave a loop, and route it through the old lamp-clip gaps rather than drilling. **On the fixed body**, a printed replacement lamp socket with four contacts and ribbon channels for NeoPixels already exists ([Thingiverse 1952690](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:1952690)); it removes the flexing wires but puts the light lower, so it is dimmer and probably more even, and no source has tested it. Either way, a printed diffuser in white, natural or clear filament pressed into the cap spreads the hot spots ([Adafruit Learn](https://learn.adafruit.com/neopixel-arcade-button/assembly)).

**The effects.** The button firmware maps the NeoHEX's 37 pixels to rings and angles for `ripple`, `rings` and `spin` (`hardware/HANDOFF.md`). A plain 12- or 16-LED ring keeps spinning and chasing effects but loses the centre-to-edge ripple. Keeping the NeoHEX in the cap, or adding a small centre cluster to a ring, preserves more of the twenty turn effects. Either way the change stays in the hardware session's `config.h` pixel map, not in the engine.

|---|---|---|

The decision then follows from the results. If a re-switched generic 100 mm dome passes the edge slaps, six of them with Omron switches is the buy, at roughly CAD 15 a dome plus the switch. If it binds, the Sanwa or Samducksa grade is worth chasing. If 100 mm crowds a Twilight Imperium table, the 60 mm takes its place. If the Big Switch arrives and survives the slaps, it is the one option that keeps both the printed caps and the internal pull-up.

## Conclusion

Bought buttons don't improve on today's button by having a better switch rating. They improve on it by giving three separate jobs to three proper parts: a steel spring lifts the cap, a snap switch clicks and closes, and a long moulded guide keeps the cap level. The printed flexures have been doing all three at once, and that is why they kept breaking. The same idea rescues the printed route too: bought switch and steel spring, printed guide only. The open question is no longer the switch's life but whether a moulded or printed guide stays straight under an edge slap, and only a hands-on test answers that.

Two trade-offs are easy to miss. A bought dome turns the button inside out: the dome becomes the top and the box becomes a printed base with the electronics beside the stem. It also gives up Garett's printable caps (lithophane, planet, system tile), which only the Big Switch and the printed-body route keep. And contact metal, not cycle count, decides long-term reliability on a 3.3 V pin: silver arcade switches want one resistor, while gold parts get by on the internal pull-up. So which button to buy partly depends on how much the custom caps matter, as well as on feel.
